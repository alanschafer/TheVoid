**Apocalyptic Literature** — Jewish and Christian writings (200 BCE–200 CE) featuring symbolic visions of cosmic conflict and divine intervention. Examples: *Daniel*, *Revelation*, 1 *Enoch*.

**Archaeological Dating** — Methods for determining the age of artifacts and structures, including radiocarbon dating, ceramic typology, and stratigraphic analysis.

**Canon/Canonical** — The official collection of biblical books recognized as authoritative scripture by religious communities. Christian canon formation occurred roughly 200–400 CE.

**Deuteronomistic History** — The narrative from *Deuteronomy* through 2 *Kings*, edited during the Babylonian exile (6th century BCE) to interpret Israel’s history through themes of covenant obedience and disobedience.

**Documentary Hypothesis** — Scholarly theory that the Pentateuch (*Genesis*–*Deuteronomy*) was compiled from four primary sources: J (Yahwist), E (Elohist), D (Deuteronomist), and P (Priestly), composed at different historical periods.

**Form Criticism** — Method of biblical analysis that examines oral traditions behind written texts, identifying literary forms (parables, miracle stories, legal codes) and their original social contexts.

**Hellenistic Period** — Era following Alexander the Great’s conquests (336–63 BCE), when Greek culture, language, and philosophy spread throughout the Mediterranean, influencing Jewish and early Christian development.

**Historical-Critical Method** — Approaches that investigate biblical texts within their original historical contexts, using archaeological evidence, comparative studies, and literary analysis.

**Masoretic Text (MT)** — The standard Hebrew text of the Hebrew Bible, preserved by Jewish scribes (Masoretes) between 600–1000 CE; forms the basis for most modern translations.

**Oral Tradition** — Period when biblical stories, laws, and teachings were transmitted verbally before being committed to writing, often adapted for different audiences and circumstances.

**Pseudepigrapha** — Jewish and Christian writings falsely attributed to biblical figures but composed later, often during the Hellenistic and Roman periods.

**Pseudonymous** — Literary works attributed to famous figures but actually written by later authors, a common practice in ancient religious literature.

**Q Source** — Hypothetical collection of Jesus’ sayings used independently by Matthew and Luke in composing their Gospels.

**Redaction Criticism** — Study of how biblical editors (redactors) shaped and arranged material to serve theological purposes, revealing the perspectives of later authors.

**Septuagint (LXX)** — Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures produced in Alexandria (250–150 BCE), used widely by Greek-speaking Jews and early Christians.

**Source Criticism** — Identification of different written sources underlying biblical texts, revealing their complex literary development.

**Synoptic Gospels** — *Matthew*, *Mark*, and *Luke*, which share similar content and structure, allowing comparison to determine literary relationships and historical development.

**Textual Criticism** — Scholarly discipline that compares ancient manuscripts to determine the most authentic reading of biblical texts, accounting for scribal errors and editorial changes.
