In 2 Kings 2:23–25, we encounter an account in the Old Testament that has drawn significant scholarly discussion:

The prophet Elisha is mocked by a group of children, and in response, he calls down a curse, after which two female bears emerge from the forest and maul 42 of them.

## The Scene

Elisha has just witnessed the miraculous ascent of his mentor Elijah into the heavens.

Now the sole prophetic figure, Elisha is walking toward Bethel—a city already known for its idolatry.

As he approaches, a group of young males come out of the city and mock him with the phrase: "Go up, you baldhead! Go up, you baldhead!"

Elisha then turns around, curses them in the name of YHWH, and two she-bears emerge from the forest and maul 42 of the youths.

What does the Hebrew actually say?

The key term is

(na'arim qetanim).

Na'ar is a term broadly used for boy, youth, or young man, but not exclusively children.

Qatan means small or young, and when combined with na'arim, it amplifies the youthfulness of the group.

What does the Septuagint say?

In the Septuagint, the phrase becomes: παιδ■ρια µικρ■

(paidaria mikra)

Paidarion is the diminutive of pais

(child/servant), meaning little child or young lad.

Mikra simply means small.

This linguistic choice has been interpreted by many scholars as referring to children possibly in the pre-adolescent age range. These are not grown men or teen hooligans—they are very likely children in the 8–10-year-old range.

Scholarly Consensus

The combination of Hebrew and Greek terminology indicates young children, not adult mockers. This aligns with other uses of na'ar qatan in Hebrew scripture:

Genesis 44:20: Benjamin, likely a child, is called a na'ar qatan

1 Samuel 20:35: A servant boy is called the same

1 Kings 3:7: Solomon calls himself a na'ar qatan—emphasizing inexperience

A number of linguistic studies and scholarly perspectives point to literal children, not metaphorical "youths" or grown troublemakers.

Conclusion

The passage is notable for its severity:

Elisha, a prophet of God, curses a group of young children, and YHWH responds by sending bears to maul 42 of them—and this act is presented without divine rebuke or regret.

The ancient texts, read plainly, point to children. This makes the story not only severe but cited in discussions on the theological and ethical dimensions of certain Old Testament narratives.
